## 1. What is the RBI?

The **Reserve Bank of India (RBI)** is India's **central bank**, not a retail commercial bank like SBI or HDFC Bank. RBI sets and implements monetary policy, regulates banking, oversees payment systems, manages currency and certain foreign-exchange functions, and acts as banker to banks and to government.

### Must-remember RBI facts

| Fact | Answer |
|---|---|
| Constituted under | **Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934** |
| Started operations | **1 April 1935** |
| Nationalised | **1 January 1949** |
| Initial central office | **Kolkata (then Calcutta)** |
| Central office moved to Mumbai | **1937** |
| Present headquarters | **Mumbai** |
| First RBI Governor | **Sir Osborne Smith** |
| First Indian RBI Governor | **C. D. Deshmukh** |
| Governor as of 31 Oct 2026 | **Sanjay Malhotra** |
| Main monetary-policy objective | **Price stability while keeping the objective of growth in mind** |

### Important RBI functions

1. **Monetary authority:** Influences short-term rates, liquidity and inflation using policy instruments.
2. **Regulator/supervisor:** Regulates and supervises eligible banks and other financial institutions.
3. **Currency issuer:** Issues Indian banknotes **except ₹1 notes**, which are issued by the Government of India. Coins are issued by the Government and put into circulation through RBI.
4. **Banker's bank:** Commercial banks maintain required cash reserves with RBI and may access RBI liquidity facilities.
5. **Banker to government:** Provides banking services and assists with government debt management.
6. **Foreign exchange management:** Manages forex reserves and performs functions under FEMA.
7. **Payment-system oversight:** Supports safe and efficient payment and settlement systems.
8. **Financial stability:** Acts as lender of last resort in the appropriate circumstances.

**Exam trap:** RBI ≠ SBI. SBI is a commercial bank; RBI is the central bank.

## 2. What is Monetary Policy?

**Monetary policy** is how RBI influences borrowing costs, money-market interest rates and liquidity, primarily to maintain price stability.

### Two broad directions

| Expansionary / accommodative tendency | Contractionary / tightening tendency |
|---|---|
| RBI may cut policy rates and/or inject liquidity | RBI may raise policy rates and/or absorb liquidity |
| Borrowing can become cheaper | Borrowing can become costlier |
| Can encourage spending and investment | Can cool excess demand and inflation pressure |
| Used when demand/growth is weak (depending on inflation outlook) | Used when inflationary pressure is high (depending on outlook) |

**Example:** Suppose an eligible floating-rate home loan has a rate of 8.50%. The policy repo rate goes up by 0.25 percentage points. If the loan benchmark and bank pricing eventually transmit the change completely, the loan rate may become 8.75%. The EMI or tenure may change based on the contract. **This transmission is not automatic or immediate for every loan.**

**Supply-shock caution:** If onion prices soar because flooding destroys crops, higher interest rates do not directly grow more onions. Monetary policy can still contain broader demand and second-round inflation effects.

### Basis points (bps)

- **100 basis points (bps) = 1 percentage point.**
- **25 bps = 0.25 percentage points.**
- Rate moving **5.25% → 5.50%** is a **25-bps increase**, NOT a 25% increase.

## 4. Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)

The **MPC** is the statutory committee that determines the **policy repo rate** required to achieve the inflation target. It operates under **Section 45ZB of the RBI Act, 1934**.

### Composition: 6 members

- **3 RBI members:** RBI Governor (chairperson), Deputy Governor in charge of monetary policy, and one RBI officer nominated by RBI's Central Board.
- **3 external members:** Appointed by the Central Government.
- **Minimum 4 meetings per year** (typically 6 scheduled).
- **Quorum: 4 members.**
- **Decision:** Majority of votes of members present and voting; the Governor has a **casting vote in a tie**.
- **Minutes:** Normally published **14 days after** the meeting.
- **Monetary Policy Report:** RBI publishes it **every six months**.

**Important distinction:** The **Central Government in consultation with RBI** notifies the inflation target. The **MPC decides the policy repo rate**, not the Union Budget and not each of CRR/SLR individually.

### Inflation target applicable for 2026–2031

- **Target:** 4% headline CPI inflation.
- **Lower tolerance:** 2%.
- **Upper tolerance:** 6%.
- **Valid period:** **1 April 2026 – 31 March 2031** (Government notification dated **25 March 2026**).

**Statutory failure test:** Average CPI inflation **above 6%** or **below 2%** for **any three consecutive quarters** constitutes failure to meet the target. RBI must submit reasons, proposed remedies and estimated recovery timeframe to the Central Government.

**Exam traps:**

- A *single month* with CPI above 6% **does not** by itself meet the three-quarter failure condition.
- Inflation goal is **4%, not 6%** (6% is the upper tolerance).
- MPC composition = **3 RBI + 3 Government-appointed external members**.

**Sources:** [Government notification](https://gazettetracker.com/g/CG-DL-E-25032026-271285); [RBI monetary-policy framework](https://systemhealth.rbi.org.in/Scripts/FS_Overview2752.aspx.html).

## 5. Repo, Reverse Repo, SDF, MSF and Bank Rate

Think of an imaginary bank, **ABC Bank**. It sometimes **needs** money and sometimes has **surplus** money.

### 5.1 Repo Rate — bank borrows liquidity from RBI

**Repo = repurchase agreement.** Under RBI repo operations, an eligible participant temporarily transfers eligible securities against cash, agreeing to reverse the deal at the agreed date/price.

**Story:**

1. ABC Bank needs **₹100 crore overnight** for short-term liquidity.
2. It provides eligible government securities under an RBI repo transaction.
3. RBI gives it ₹100 crore liquidity.
4. The bank reverses the agreement as scheduled and pays the financing cost.

**Simple interest illustration** (₹100 crore, 5.50% annualised, 1 day):

`Interest ≈ ₹100 crore × 0.055 × (1 / 365) = ₹1.507 lakh`

Actual market settlement conventions may differ; the goal is understanding the scale of overnight interest.

**Effect:** Repo operation **injects** liquidity into the banking system. Repo rate rising makes this type of funding costlier.

**Memory:** **Repo → RBI gives money to bank (against securities).**

### 5.2 Reverse Repo — RBI absorbs surplus liquidity

**Story:** ABC Bank has surplus ₹100 crore. In a reverse repo with RBI, it gives funds to RBI, receives eligible securities as collateral under the transaction, and receives funds/interest on reversal.

**Effect:** Reverse repo **absorbs** liquidity.

**Key distinction:** The **fixed reverse repo rate stopped being the LAF corridor floor in April 2022**. **SDF** is the present floor.

### 5.3 Standing Deposit Facility (SDF)

SDF allows eligible participants to place **overnight uncollateralised deposits** with RBI.

**Story:** ABC Bank has extra ₹100 crore and places it in SDF overnight. It earns the SDF rate, but **RBI does not give government securities as collateral**.

| Reverse repo | SDF |
|---|---|
| Bank supplies funds to RBI | Bank supplies funds to RBI |
| RBI provides collateral securities | **RBI does NOT provide collateral** |
| Absorbs liquidity | Absorbs liquidity |
| Fixed reverse repo is not the LAF floor | **SDF is the LAF floor** |

**SDF introduced:** **8 April 2022**. It removes the collateral constraint on RBI's capacity to absorb liquidity.

**Source:** [RBI SDF introduction](https://systemhealth.rbi.org.in/Scripts/BS_PressReleaseDisplay.aspx_prid%3D53536.html).

### 5.4 Marginal Standing Facility (MSF)

MSF is a standing overnight borrowing option for eligible banks, subject to RBI's limits and collateral rules. Banks may access liquidity by using eligible securities, including permitted dipping into SLR holdings.

**Story:** ABC Bank suddenly needs additional ₹100 crore overnight. It can use MSF, subject to eligibility and collateral, even though the rate is higher than the policy repo rate.

- Repo: regular policy-rate collateralised liquidity operations.
- MSF: additional overnight liquidity safety valve, at a higher rate.
- **MSF is NOT unsecured lending.**

**Memory:** **MSF → bank urgently needs more funds → RBI can provide liquidity against eligible securities.**

### 5.5 Bank Rate

Bank Rate is a **statutory benchmark**, historically linked to RBI's rediscounting function and currently relevant for certain regulatory/penal interest calculations. It is aligned with the **MSF rate** in the present arrangement.

**Do not confuse:** Bank Rate is **not** simply the interest rate at which a commercial bank lends to its home-loan customers.

### 5.6 Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF) corridor

The operational corridor guides overnight money-market interest rates:

```text
Upper bound  → MSF       5.75%
                  ↑ 25 bps
Centre       → Repo      5.50%
                  ↓ 25 bps
Lower bound  → SDF       5.25%
```

- RBI aims to keep the **weighted average call rate (WACR)** aligned with the policy repo rate through liquidity management.
- The corridor influences market rates; it is **not an absolute promise** that all transactions always occur within those rates.

### All five at a glance

| Instrument | Does bank give cash to RBI or receive cash? | Security/collateral? | Main purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Repo | **Receives** | Bank provides eligible securities | RBI liquidity injection |
| Reverse repo | **Gives** | RBI provides securities | RBI liquidity absorption |
| SDF | **Gives** | RBI provides **none** | Absorb surplus liquidity |
| MSF | **Receives** | Bank provides eligible securities | Additional overnight liquidity |
| Bank Rate | Not a separate everyday deposit/loan operation like above | — | Statutory/reference rate |

## 6. CRR and SLR

Both are measured with reference to **Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL)**. Do **not** assume NDTL is always exactly equal to gross deposits.

### 6.1 CRR — Cash Reserve Ratio

A required proportion of applicable NDTL must be maintained as a **cash balance with RBI**. RBI does **not** pay interest on required CRR balances.

**Example:** ABC Bank NDTL = ₹1,000 crore, CRR = 3%.

`Required CRR = 1,000 × 3/100 = ₹30 crore`

If CRR increases to 4%:

`New CRR = ₹40 crore; additional reserve = ₹10 crore`

**Typical effect:** Higher CRR → less deployable bank liquidity, other things equal. Lower CRR → frees some liquidity.

**Legal reference:** RBI Act, 1934, Section 42 (for scheduled banks; bank-category regulations may matter).

### 6.2 SLR — Statutory Liquidity Ratio

Banks maintain a required share of applicable NDTL as **eligible liquid assets** (such as cash, gold and approved securities, often government securities), in accordance with regulation.

**Example:** ABC Bank NDTL = ₹1,000 crore, SLR = 18%.

`Required SLR assets = 1,000 × 18/100 = ₹180 crore`

**Why useful?** Maintains liquidity buffers and holdings of relatively liquid/high-quality eligible assets.

**Legal reference:** Banking Regulation Act, 1949, Section 24.

| CRR | SLR |
|---|---|
| Required **cash balance with RBI** | Required **eligible liquid-asset holdings** |
| No RBI interest on mandatory CRR balance | Government securities in SLR portfolio can earn coupon |
| Reserve cash not available for ordinary bank use | Securities may be usable subject to applicable rules |
| Current reference: **3%** | Current reference: **18%** |

**Trap:** If CRR=3% and SLR=18%, you **cannot automatically say the bank is legally free to lend the remaining 79%**. Capital, liquidity, asset-quality and other prudential rules also apply. The bank balance sheet is not a simplistic cash-in/cash-out piggy bank.

## 7. Open Market Operations (OMO)

**OMO = RBI's outright buying or selling of government securities in the market to manage liquidity.**

### RBI buys G-Secs (OMO purchase)

- ABC Bank holds government bonds.
- RBI buys ₹500 crore of those bonds from the bank.
- RBI pays cash/reserve balances to the bank; bank gives up securities.
- **Banking-system liquidity rises**, before offsetting operations.

**Rule:** **RBI buys bonds → injects liquidity.**

### RBI sells G-Secs (OMO sale)

- RBI sells ₹500 crore of bonds.
- Bank or other buyer pays RBI.
- **System liquidity falls**, before offsetting operations.

**Rule:** **RBI sells bonds → absorbs liquidity.**

### Repo vs outright OMO

| Repo | OMO outright buy/sell |
|---|---|
| Temporary transaction with agreed repurchase/reversal | Outright security transfer, no automatic reverse leg |
| Typically short-term liquidity management | Can affect more durable liquidity |
| Repo example: bank receives cash temporarily | OMO purchase: RBI acquires government bond outright |

**Exam trap:** RBI buying securities from a bank through OMO does **not** mean the government has newly borrowed money. The outstanding bond simply changes hands.

## 8. Bonds — How do they work? Are they loans?

### 8.1 What is a bond?

A **bond is a tradable debt instrument**: an issuer (Government/company) borrows money from investors and promises repayment under contract terms, commonly with interest (coupon).

- **Issuer** = borrower.
- **Bondholder** = creditor/lender.
- **Face value** = principal amount stated on bond.
- **Coupon rate** = interest relative to face value.
- **Maturity** = date principal is repayable (unless other terms apply).
- **Market price** = price at which investors buy/sell the bond (can differ from face value).
- **Yield** = return relative to price/cash flows, **not always equal to coupon rate**.

### 8.2 Example: five-year bond

Suppose Government issues a hypothetical **₹10,000 face value bond**, **7% annual coupon**, **5-year maturity**. For easy calculation, assume annual coupon payment and purchase at par.

`Annual coupon = 10,000 × 7% = ₹700`

| Year | Coupon received | Principal received |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1 | ₹700 | — |
| 2 | ₹700 | — |
| 3 | ₹700 | — |
| 4 | ₹700 | — |
| 5 | ₹700 | ₹10,000 |
| **Total** | **₹3,500** | **₹10,000** |

Your combined cash received over five years is ₹13,500, including the return of ₹10,000 principal. Indian dated government securities commonly pay coupons **half-yearly**; annual coupon above is a learning simplification.

### 8.3 Loan vs bond

| Conventional bank loan | Bond |
|---|---|
| Borrower gets money from lender/bank | Issuer raises funds from one or more investors |
| Loan agreement | Transferable debt security (for many bonds) |
| Often EMI or other repayment schedule | Often coupon payments + redemption at maturity |
| Commonly less straightforward to transfer | Many bonds trade in secondary markets |
| Can be fixed or floating interest | Can be fixed, floating or zero-coupon |

**Key idea:** Both create a debt obligation, but the legal and market structures differ. Buying a bond does **not** make you an equity owner.

### 8.4 Bond price and yield move inversely

For a **fixed-coupon** bond:

- Market yields/interest rates **rise** → old bond **price generally falls**.
- Market yields/interest rates **fall** → old bond **price generally rises**.

**Why?** If new bonds offer better yields, an older low-coupon bond needs a lower purchase price to compete.

**One-year remaining example:** Assume bond pays exactly ₹10,700 one year from today (₹10,000 principal + ₹700 final coupon).

- At an 8% required annual yield: `Price = 10,700 / 1.08 ≈ ₹9,907.41`.
- At a 6% required annual yield: `Price = 10,700 / 1.06 ≈ ₹10,094.34`.

This simplified present-value example ignores accrued interest and fees. **The ₹700 coupon doesn't change just because market price changes** (for a fixed-rate bond).

### 8.5 Important bond types

| Instrument | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **G-Sec** | Central/State government debt security |
| **Dated Government Security** | Central government bond, typically longer-term |
| **State Development Loan (SDL)** | State government bond |
| **Corporate bond** | Company-issued debt instrument; carries issuer credit risk |
| **Treasury Bill (T-Bill)** | Short-term central government security, **zero coupon**, issued at discount |
| **Floating Rate Bond** | Coupon varies according to reference terms |
| **Zero-Coupon Bond** | No periodic coupon; return may come from difference between purchase and redemption price |

### 8.6 Treasury Bills (very exam-relevant)

Standard Indian T-Bill tenors: **91, 182 and 364 days**. T-Bills are typically sold at a **discount** and redeemed at **face value**.

**Example:** A ₹100 face value T-Bill bought for ₹97.50 pays ₹100 at maturity.

`Investor's gross difference = ₹100 − ₹97.50 = ₹2.50`

No separate periodic coupon is paid.

### 8.7 Connecting bonds to CRR, SLR, Repo and OMO

1. **Government issues bond** to raise funds (primary market).
2. **Bank buys bond** and may hold it as an eligible SLR security.
3. **Bank uses eligible bond in repo/MSF** to get temporary liquidity (subject to eligibility and operational rules).
4. **RBI buys existing bond via OMO** to inject liquidity; the Government's underlying debt remains outstanding.

**Risk caution:** Domestic-currency central government securities carry extremely low default risk, but **market price and interest-rate risk remain**, especially if sold before maturity. Corporate bonds additionally have meaningful issuer credit risk.

**Source:** [RBI — Government Securities Market in India: A Primer](https://m.rbi.org.in/commonman/english/scripts/FAQs.aspx?Id=711).

## 9. Inflation — types, effects and RBI response

**Inflation** is a sustained increase in the **overall price level** for a basket of goods/services, not simply a rise in one item’s price.

### 9.1 Inflation calculation

`Inflation (%) = [(Current price index − Previous-period index) / Previous-period index] × 100`

**Example:** Basket price increases ₹5,000 → ₹5,500:

`Inflation = (500 / 5,000) × 100 = 10%`

**Purchasing power:** With fixed money, higher price levels mean you can buy fewer goods/services.

### 9.2 Inflation vs disinflation vs deflation

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Inflation** | Prices generally rise | 100 → 110 |
| **Disinflation** | Positive inflation slows | Inflation rate 10% → 5%; prices still rise |
| **Deflation** | Overall price level falls | 100 → 95 |
| **Stagflation** | High inflation with weak/stagnant growth and generally high unemployment | Cost shock alongside weak economy |

**Crucial trap:** Inflation falling **does not necessarily mean prices are falling**. If prices were ₹100, then ₹110, then ₹115: inflation first 10%, then ~4.55%; prices still increased.

### 9.3 Demand-pull inflation

**Excess aggregate demand relative to economy's supply capacity** pushes up overall prices.

**Simple story:** Too many buyers chasing limited output. RBI may raise policy rates to slow credit growth, spending and demand.

### 9.4 Cost-push inflation

**Rising production/input costs or supply constraints** push prices up.

**Simple story:** Crude oil becomes scarce/expensive → transportation and factory costs rise → final prices rise. RBI cannot directly produce more oil, though monetary tightening may restrain broader inflation pressures.

| Demand-pull | Cost-push |
|---|---|
| Driven by excess spending/demand | Driven by higher input costs or restricted supply |
| Policy tightening can more directly cool demand | Supply fixes may need non-monetary policy measures |

### 9.5 Other useful terms

- **Headline inflation:** Overall CPI inflation across included basket items.
- **Core inflation:** Underlying measure often excluding volatile categories such as food and fuel (exact exclusions vary by measure).
- **Imported inflation:** Higher prices caused by pricier imported goods or depreciating domestic currency, other things equal.

## 10. CPI vs WPI — updated facts for the 2026 exam

### 10.1 CPI (Consumer Price Index)

- Measures price changes for **selected goods and services consumed by households**.
- Includes **services**.
- Compiled/released by **National Statistics Office (NSO), MoSPI**.
- India's statutory inflation target refers to **headline CPI**, not WPI.
- **Current CPI base year: 2024 = 100**.
- The revised series was released on **12 February 2026**, replacing **2012 = 100**.

**Why 'base year = 100'?** It is an index reference, **not** a statement that goods cost ₹100 in that year.

### 10.2 WPI (Wholesale Price Index)

- Measures movements in prices of **selected goods at wholesale/producer-related stages**; not a comprehensive consumer-services basket.
- Published by **Office of the Economic Adviser, DPIIT, Ministry of Commerce and Industry**.
- **Current WPI base year: 2022–23**.
- The revised WPI series was released on **15 June 2026**, replacing **2011–12**.

### CPI vs WPI summary

| Feature | CPI | WPI |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Prices paid/experienced by consumers | Wholesale prices of selected goods |
| Services covered? | **Yes** | Not as a general services basket (separate PPIs introduced in 2026) |
| Publisher | **NSO / MoSPI** | **Office of Economic Adviser / DPIIT** |
| Current base year | **2024 = 100** | **2022–23** |
| Used for RBI headline inflation target? | **Yes** | **No** |

**Exam trap:** 2012 (CPI) and 2011–12 (WPI) are **old** base years for the 2026 exam.

**Official sources:** [MoSPI/PIB CPI 2024 series](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2227012&lang=1&reg=3); [DPIIT/PIB WPI 2022–23 series](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2272872&lang=1&reg=3).

## 11. Monetary policy transmission: one consolidated example

Assume the economy has **demand-driven inflation** and RBI wants to cool demand.

1. **MPC increases policy repo rate.**
2. Short-term bank funding conditions may tighten.
3. Lending benchmarks and deposit rates may adjust gradually.
4. Loans can become more expensive for some borrowers.
5. Borrowers may defer purchases; companies may delay investment.
6. Aggregate demand may weaken, easing inflation pressure over time.

RBI can additionally adjust liquidity through **repo operations, SDF, CRR and OMO** under their respective frameworks. **Policy-rate decisions and liquidity operations are related but distinct.**

Reverse direction (weak demand + manageable inflation): RBI may lower rates or inject liquidity to support economic activity, while balancing inflation and financial-stability risks.

## 13. Numericals — solved examples

### Q1. CRR

**Question:** Bank NDTL = ₹2,500 crore; CRR = 4%. Required balance?  
**Solution:** `2,500 × 0.04 = ₹100 crore`.

### Q2. Additional CRR after hike

**Question:** NDTL = ₹10,000 crore; CRR rises 3% → 5%. Additional balance?  
**Solution:** Difference = 2 percentage points. `10,000 × 0.02 = ₹200 crore`.

### Q3. SLR

**Question:** NDTL = ₹1,800 crore; SLR = 18%. Required holdings?  
**Solution:** `1,800 × 0.18 = ₹324 crore`.

### Q4. Coupon

**Question:** Face value ₹50,000; fixed annual coupon 8%. Annual coupon?  
**Solution:** `50,000 × 0.08 = ₹4,000`.

### Q5. Current yield (not YTM)

**Question:** Annual coupon ₹700; bond market price ₹9,500. Current yield?  
**Solution:** `700 / 9,500 × 100 ≈ 7.37%`.

### Q6. CPI inflation

**Question:** CPI index 120 last year; 126 this year. Year-on-year inflation?  
**Solution:** `(126 − 120) / 120 × 100 = 5%`.

### Q7. Basis points

**Question:** Repo goes from 5.25% to 5.50%. Rise in bps?  
**Solution:** 0.25 percentage points = **25 bps**.

### Q8. Treasury Bill discount

**Question:** T-Bill purchased at ₹97.80 and redeemed for ₹100; gross gain per ₹100 face value?  
**Solution:** `100 − 97.80 = ₹2.20` (not an annualised yield).

## 15. One-page quick revision sheet (read in 5 minutes)

### Historical facts

- **RBI Act:** 1934 | **Founded:** 1 April 1935 | **Nationalised:** 1 January 1949.
- **First Governor:** Sir Osborne Smith | **First Indian Governor:** C. D. Deshmukh.
- **Headquarters:** Mumbai.
- **MPC:** 6 members; 3 from RBI + 3 external; Governor chairs; quorum 4; minutes after 14 days.

### Rate snapshot — dated 7 October 2026

- **Repo 5.50% → SDF 5.25% → MSF 5.75% → Bank Rate 5.75%.**
- **CRR 3.00%, SLR 18.00%, fixed reverse repo 3.35%** (RBI snapshot from September 2026).
- **Next MPC meeting:** 2–4 December 2026; refresh rates before exam.

### Which way does money flow?

- **Repo/MSF:** RBI → bank (liquidity injection).
- **Reverse Repo/SDF:** bank → RBI (liquidity absorption).
- **SDF vs Reverse Repo:** RBI provides **no collateral** under SDF.
- **OMO buy:** RBI pays market → liquidity **up**.
- **OMO sell:** market pays RBI → liquidity **down**.

### CRR / SLR

- **CRR:** cash balance with RBI; no interest on mandatory reserve.
- **SLR:** eligible liquid assets such as cash/gold/approved government securities.
- Formula: `NDTL × required percentage / 100`.

### Bonds

- **Issuer borrows; investor lends.** Coupon ≠ yield.
- **Yield ↑ → fixed-rate bond price ↓**, and vice versa.
- **T-Bills:** 91/182/364 days; zero coupon; discount → face value.

### Inflation

- **Inflation:** rising general prices.
- **Disinflation:** prices rising more slowly.
- **Deflation:** general prices fall.
- **Demand-pull:** excess aggregate demand.
- **Cost-push:** rising input costs/supply shock.
- **CPI:** NSO/MoSPI; **2024=100**; includes services; RBI targeting index.
- **WPI:** OEA/DPIIT; **2022–23**; wholesale goods.
- **Inflation target:** CPI **4%**, tolerance **2–6%**, April 2026–March 2031.

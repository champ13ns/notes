# Day 5 — Basel Norms, Capital Adequacy & Banking Risk Management

**IBPS RRB Scale II IT Officer | Financial Awareness**  
**Notes updated: 26 November 2026**

## 1. Why banks need capital

Banks accept deposits and lend money. Loans carry credit risk, markets move, systems fail, and depositors may withdraw funds. Bank capital acts as a loss-absorbing buffer and protects depositors and financial stability.

**Capital is not the same as deposits.** Deposits are liabilities the bank owes customers; capital is the owners' / regulatory loss-absorbing funding.

---

## 2. Basel Committee & Basel Accords

- **BCBS:** Basel Committee on Banking Supervision.
- Established in **1974** by central-bank and supervisory authorities of G10 countries following banking disturbances.
- Secretariat hosted by the Bank for International Settlements (BIS), Basel, Switzerland.
- It develops international supervisory standards; it is not a world central bank and does not directly regulate Indian banks.
- RBI implements applicable Basel standards through domestic regulations.

### Basel timeline

| Framework | Year / period | Key focus |
|---|---|---|
| Basel I | 1988 | Capital against credit risk; 8% minimum capital ratio |
| Basel II | 2004 | Three pillars; more risk-sensitive capital framework |
| Basel III | 2010 onward | Stronger capital quality, buffers, leverage and liquidity standards |

**Trap:** Basel III was developed after the Global Financial Crisis (2007–09), but implementation was phased in.

---

## 3. Basel II — Three Pillars

| Pillar | Focus | Easy memory |
|---|---|---|
| Pillar 1 | Minimum capital requirements for credit, market and operational risk | Minimum capital |
| Pillar 2 | Supervisory review and bank's internal capital adequacy process | Supervisor checks |
| Pillar 3 | Market discipline through disclosures/transparency | Public disclosure |

**Pillar 1** gives minimum quantitative requirements.  
**Pillar 2** looks at whether capital is adequate for risks beyond the minimum and whether supervision is effective.  
**Pillar 3** lets investors, depositors and analysts evaluate the bank through disclosures.

## 4. CRAR / Capital Adequacy Ratio

**CRAR = Regulatory Capital ÷ Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA) × 100**

It measures capital available to absorb losses relative to the riskiness of exposures.

### Example
Bank's eligible regulatory capital = ₹80 crore  
RWA = ₹800 crore

CRAR = 80 / 800 × 100 = **10%**

If capital stays unchanged and RWA increases, CRAR falls. If the bank raises capital while RWA stays the same, CRAR rises.

### What are Risk-Weighted Assets?
Exposure values are adjusted using risk weights based on regulatory risk. A ₹100 exposure to a counterparty with a 100% risk weight contributes ₹100 RWA; at 50% risk weight it contributes ₹50 RWA, before other applicable adjustments.

RWA can reflect:
- Credit risk
- Market risk
- Operational risk
- Relevant off-balance-sheet exposures converted/weighted as prescribed

**Trap:** RWA is not simply total assets or total loans.

## 5. Regulatory capital layers

### CET1 — Common Equity Tier 1
Highest-quality capital: common equity, eligible reserves and retained earnings, subject to regulatory deductions/adjustments.

### AT1 — Additional Tier 1
Eligible perpetual instruments and other qualifying items that meet Basel/RBI criteria. Must have loss-absorption features; not all bank bonds qualify.

### Tier 1 Capital
**CET1 + AT1**

It absorbs losses while the bank remains a going concern.

### Tier 2 Capital
Eligible supplementary capital instruments, provisions and other qualifying items, subject to regulatory limits/conditions. Generally supports loss absorption in winding-up / gone-concern scenarios.

### Total Regulatory Capital
**Tier 1 + Tier 2**, after regulatory adjustments.

| Capital | Quality / role |
|---|---|
| CET1 | Highest-quality going-concern capital |
| AT1 | Additional going-concern capital |
| Tier 1 | CET1 + AT1 |
| Tier 2 | Supplementary / gone-concern capital |
| Total | Tier 1 + Tier 2 |

**Trap:** Tier 1 is not CET1 alone; it includes eligible AT1.

## 6. Basel III minimum capital — India exam figures

For a regular scheduled commercial bank (commonly tested RBI Basel III framework), base minimums and capital buffers combine as follows:

| Requirement | Minimum |
|---|---:|
| CET1 minimum | 5.5% of RWA |
| Tier 1 minimum | 7.0% |
| Total CRAR minimum | 9.0% |
| Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB) | 2.5% |
| CET1 including CCB | 8.0% |
| Tier 1 including CCB | 9.5% |
| Total CRAR including CCB | 11.5% |

**CCB** is built in good times and is intended to be drawn down in stress. Falling into the buffer may trigger restrictions on discretionary distributions, such as dividends and bonuses, under the regulatory framework.

### Institution-specific capital
- RRBs: minimum CRAR commonly tested as **9%**; do not mechanically apply the commercial-bank CCB stack.
- Small Finance Banks and Payments Banks: minimum CRAR commonly **15%** under applicable RBI framework.
- Requirements may differ by bank category, transitional provisions and current RBI circulars—check the exact applicable rule for current affairs questions.

## 7. Leverage Ratio

Basel III leverage ratio is a non-risk-based backstop to prevent excessive balance-sheet leverage.

Conceptually:

**Leverage Ratio = Tier 1 Capital ÷ Exposure Measure**

Unlike CRAR, the denominator is not risk-weighted in the same way. It is intended to limit build-up of leverage even where risk weights look low.

**CRAR vs Leverage Ratio**
- CRAR: risk-weighted measure.
- Leverage ratio: non-risk-weighted backstop.

---

## 8. Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB)

- Buffer = **2.5% of RWA** in the standard Basel III framework.
- It is held above minimum capital requirements.
- Banks can use it during stress, but distribution restrictions may apply when buffer levels fall.
- It helps banks conserve capital in normal periods to withstand losses later.

**Exam trap:** CCB is a buffer on top of the minimum requirement; it is not the same as the minimum CRAR itself.

## 9. Banking risk categories

### Credit Risk
Borrower/counterparty fails to pay principal, interest or other obligation.

Examples: NPA, default, counterparty failure.

Controls: credit scoring, underwriting, collateral, limits, diversification, monitoring, provisioning.

### Market Risk
Loss due to changes in market prices/rates.

Includes:
- Interest-rate risk
- Foreign-exchange risk
- Equity-price risk
- Commodity-price risk

Example: bond value falls when market yields rise.

### Operational Risk
Loss resulting from inadequate/failed internal processes, people, systems, or external events.

Examples:
- Cyberattack
- Fraud
- Incorrect transaction processing
- System outage
- Human error
- Vendor failure

Basel operational risk definition includes legal risk, but generally excludes strategic and reputational risk from the formal definition.

### Liquidity Risk
Bank cannot meet obligations when due without unacceptable loss.

Example: customers withdraw deposits faster than the bank can mobilise liquid assets.

### Interest Rate Risk in Banking Book (IRRBB)
Changes in interest rates affect the economic value of banking-book positions and/or net interest income.

### Concentration Risk
Too much exposure to one borrower, sector, geography, product or funding source.

### Compliance / Legal Risk
Loss, penalty or litigation from failure to comply with laws, rules or contracts.

## 10. Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)

**LCR = Stock of High-Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA) ÷ Total Net Cash Outflows over next 30 calendar days**

General Basel minimum: **100%** (subject to framework and permitted stress usage).

Purpose: ensure bank holds enough liquid assets to survive a short-term liquidity stress scenario.

HQLA typically includes eligible cash, central-bank reserves and qualifying high-quality securities, subject to haircuts, caps and eligibility conditions.

Example:
HQLA = ₹120 crore  
30-day net cash outflows = ₹100 crore

LCR = 120 / 100 × 100 = **120%**

### HQLA
High-Quality Liquid Assets: assets that can be converted into cash readily with little loss of value during stress, subject to regulatory eligibility.

---

## 11. Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR)

**NSFR = Available Stable Funding (ASF) ÷ Required Stable Funding (RSF)**

General Basel minimum: **100%**.

It promotes stable funding over a longer horizon (commonly one year), reducing reliance on unstable short-term funding.

- ASF: capital and liabilities weighted according to their stability.
- RSF: assets/off-balance-sheet exposures weighted according to liquidity and funding needs.

### LCR vs NSFR
| LCR | NSFR |
|---|---|
| Short-term liquidity stress | Structural / longer-term funding stability |
| 30-day horizon | About 1-year structural horizon |
| HQLA ÷ net cash outflows | ASF ÷ RSF |
| Minimum generally 100% | Minimum generally 100% |

## 12. Stress testing & ICAAP

### Stress Testing
A bank estimates how its capital, liquidity, profits or risk exposures would behave under adverse scenarios.

Examples:
- Interest rates rise sharply.
- Major borrowers default.
- Deposits withdraw rapidly.
- Currency depreciates.
- Cyber incident disrupts payments.

### ICAAP
**Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process**: bank's own assessment of capital needed for its risk profile, strategy and internal stress scenarios, within the supervisory framework.

**Pillar 2 connection:** ICAAP and supervisory review are core concepts associated with Pillar 2.

## 13. Risk management controls — backend/IT officer examples

| Risk / issue | IT control |
|---|---|
| Duplicate transfer | Idempotency key + unique transaction reference |
| Unauthorised access | MFA, least privilege, role-based access |
| Data alteration | Immutable audit trail, integrity checks |
| API abuse | Rate limits, monitoring, fraud rules |
| System outage | HA, DR, backups, tested recovery |
| Fraudulent transactions | Behavioural analytics, alerts, velocity limits |
| Reconciliation gap | Ledger-vs-settlement reconciliation, exception queue |
| Vendor outage | Vendor risk assessment, fallback plan, SLA monitoring |

**Key idea:** IT controls reduce operational risk; they do not replace credit or liquidity risk management.

## 14. Exam traps / rapid revision

1. **CRAR denominator = RWA**, not total deposits.
2. Basel Committee is hosted by BIS; it does not directly regulate Indian banks.
3. Basel II = three pillars.
4. Basel III strengthened capital quality, buffers, leverage and liquidity.
5. CET1 is highest quality; Tier 1 = CET1 + AT1.
6. Total capital = Tier 1 + Tier 2.
7. CCB = 2.5%; standard commercial bank CRAR stack commonly 9% + 2.5% = 11.5%.
8. LCR is 30-day liquidity; NSFR is structural funding.
9. LCR and NSFR minimum generally 100%.
10. Credit risk ≠ operational risk.
11. Leverage ratio is a non-risk-based backstop.
12. Operational risk covers processes, people, systems and external events.

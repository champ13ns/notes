## 1. What is a Financial Market?

A **financial market** is a marketplace (physical or electronic) where financial assets such as money-market instruments, shares, bonds, currencies and derivatives are issued, bought or sold.

### Why financial markets are important
- Connect savers/investors with borrowers.
- Help businesses and governments raise funds.
- Provide liquidity—investors can sell assets before maturity (where a market exists).
- Help discover prices and interest rates.
- Enable risk management and investment diversification.
- Support monetary-policy transmission and economic growth.

### Main types

| Market | Main purpose | Typical instruments |
|---|---|---|
| Money Market | Short-term borrowing/lending, generally up to 1 year | Call money, T-Bills, CP, CD, repo |
| Capital Market | Medium/long-term funding and investment | Shares, bonds, debentures, long-term G-Secs |
| Equity Market | Ownership capital | Shares |
| Debt Market | Borrowed funds | Bonds, debentures, G-Secs |
| Forex Market | Currency exchange | USD/INR, EUR/INR, etc. |
| Derivatives Market | Risk transfer / price exposure | Futures, options, swaps |

**Mnemonic:** Money market = short-term liquidity; capital market = long-term capital.

## 2. Money Market

The money market deals in short-term financial instruments, typically with original maturity **up to one year**. It helps banks, government, financial institutions and eligible corporates manage temporary liquidity needs.

### Money-market characteristics
- Short maturity.
- Generally high liquidity.
- Lower duration/price sensitivity than long-dated bonds.
- Can be secured or unsecured.
- Used for liquidity and working-capital management, not primarily long-term project finance.

### Segments
1. **Uncollateralised:** call, notice and term money (subject to eligible participants and limits).
2. **Collateralised:** repo, triparty repo/TREPS and other permitted secured arrangements.
3. **Short-term instruments:** T-Bills, CP, CD and other eligible instruments.

---

## 3. Call Money, Notice Money & Term Money

| Instrument | Tenor | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| Call Money | Overnight / 1 day | Unsecured borrowing/lending |
| Notice Money | More than 1 day up to 14 days | Unsecured |
| Term Money | More than 14 days up to 1 year | Unsecured |

These markets help participants manage short-term liquidity mismatches.

**Exam trap:** Call money is overnight; notice money is 2–14 days; term money is more than 14 days and up to 1 year (as commonly stated in the RBI framework).

## 4. Treasury Bills (T-Bills)

**Treasury Bills are short-term debt instruments issued by the Government of India to raise funds.**

### Key features
- Issued by Government of India through auctions conducted by RBI.
- Short-term money-market instruments.
- Zero-coupon: no periodic interest payment.
- Issued at a discount to face value and redeemed at face value.
- Generally regarded as having negligible domestic sovereign credit risk; however, an investor can face market-price risk if selling before maturity.

### Standard tenors
- 91 days
- 182 days
- 364 days

Special/intermediate 14-day T-Bills may also be used for State Government cash-management purposes under the framework. For regular exam questions, the standard three tenors are the core list.

### Example
Face value = ₹100  
Issue price = ₹98.20  
At maturity, investor receives ₹100.

Return = ₹100 − ₹98.20 = **₹1.80**

The T-Bill earns through discount, not a coupon.

### Simple annualised yield illustration
Investment-based yield:

\[
\text{Yield} \approx \frac{FV-P}{P}\times\frac{365}{Days}\times100
\]

For ₹98.20 purchase price, ₹100 face value and 91 days:

\[
\frac{1.80}{98.20}\times\frac{365}{91}\times100 \approx 7.35\%
\]

Discount rate using face value as denominator is different:

\[
\text{Discount rate}=\frac{FV-P}{FV}\times\frac{365}{Days}\times100
\]

≈ 7.21%. **Do not confuse discount rate with investment yield.** Actual market/auction yield conventions should be followed for official calculations.

## 5. Cash Management Bills (CMBs)

- Short-term Government of India instrument.
- Introduced to meet temporary cash-flow mismatches of the Central Government.
- Similar in general character to T-Bills.
- Issued for maturities **less than 91 days**.
- Issued on an ad-hoc basis as needed rather than on the regular T-Bill schedule.

**T-Bill vs CMB:** T-Bills have standard tenors; CMBs are used for temporary cash needs and can have shorter, non-standard maturity.

---

## 6. Commercial Paper (CP)

**Commercial Paper is an unsecured, negotiable short-term debt instrument issued in the form of a promissory note.**

### Key features
- Short-term corporate/eligible-entity borrowing.
- Unsecured: generally no collateral is pledged.
- Issuers may include eligible corporates, NBFCs, primary dealers and financial institutions as permitted by RBI.
- Typical maturity: **7 days to 1 year**.
- Often issued at a discount and redeemed at face value.
- Negotiable/tradable, subject to applicable rules.
- Issuer credit risk matters; CP is not sovereign-guaranteed.

### Example
Company issues CP with face value ₹10 lakh for ₹9.80 lakh, due in 90 days. Investor earns the ₹20,000 difference if held to maturity (before tax/transaction costs and subject to issuer repayment).

**Why issue CP?** Eligible companies may use it to meet short-term working-capital/funding requirements.

---

## 7. Certificate of Deposit (CD)

A **Certificate of Deposit** is a negotiable, unsecured money-market instrument issued by eligible banks and specified financial institutions to mobilise short-term funds.

### Common exam features
- Issued by eligible banks and select financial institutions.
- Bank CDs: generally maturity **7 days to 1 year** under the framework.
- May be issued at a discount or on other permitted rate terms.
- Negotiable; can be traded in permitted secondary markets.
- Not the same as an ordinary fixed deposit: CD is a negotiable market instrument.

### CD vs Fixed Deposit
| Certificate of Deposit | Ordinary Fixed Deposit |
|---|---|
| Negotiable market instrument | Usually non-negotiable bank deposit contract |
| Market-tradable subject to rules | Premature withdrawal normally governed by bank terms |
| Money-market instrument | Retail deposit product |
| Issued under CD regulatory framework | Bank's deposit product |

**Trap:** A CD is a *certificate of deposit*, but that does not mean every customer FD is a CD instrument.

---

## 8. Commercial Paper vs Certificate of Deposit vs T-Bill

| Feature | CP | CD | T-Bill |
|---|---|---|---|
| Issuer | Eligible corporates/entities | Eligible banks/FIs | Government of India |
| Security | Unsecured | Unsecured | Sovereign obligation |
| Main purpose | Short-term corporate funding | Short-term bank/FI funding | Government short-term borrowing |
| Typical maturity | 7 days–1 year | Banks: 7 days–1 year | 91/182/364 days |
| Return | Usually discount / issue terms | Discount or permitted rate | Discount; no coupon |
| Main risk | Issuer credit risk | Issuer bank/FI risk | Negligible sovereign credit risk; market risk if sold early |

**Memory hook:** CP = Company Paper; CD = bank certificate; T-Bill = Government short-term bill.

## 9. Repo & Triparty Repo (TREPS)

### Repo (Repurchase Agreement)
A transaction in which one party sells securities and agrees to repurchase them at a specified future date and price.

**Economic meaning:** The seller is borrowing funds using securities as collateral; the buyer/lender provides funds and receives collateral.

### Reverse repo
From the other party's perspective, the same transaction is a reverse repo: lending funds against securities with an agreement to sell them back.

**Perspective trap:** Repo and reverse repo describe the same two-legged transaction from opposite sides.

### TREPS / Triparty Repo
- A repo transaction facilitated by a third-party agent.
- Agent helps with collateral selection, settlement, custody/management and operational functions.
- In India, CCIL acts as triparty agent for TREPS.
- Collateralised short-term liquidity management.

### Repo vs CP
- Repo: collateralised borrowing.
- CP: unsecured promissory-note borrowing.

## 10. Capital Market

Capital market facilitates medium- and long-term financing, investment and trading in securities.

### Main segments
1. **Equity market:** ownership shares.
2. **Debt market:** bonds, debentures, government securities and other debt.

### Primary Market
New securities are issued to raise fresh capital.

Examples:
- IPO — Initial Public Offering (company offers shares to public for the first time).
- FPO — Further Public Offering (already listed company offers additional shares).
- Rights issue — offers additional shares to existing shareholders, usually in proportion to their holdings.
- Private placement — securities issued to a selected group of investors under applicable rules.
- Bond issuance — government/corporate raises debt.

### Secondary Market
Existing securities are traded between investors. The issuer generally does not receive fresh funds from each secondary-market trade.

Example: You buy an already-listed share from another investor on an exchange; the company is not receiving that sale amount.

**Primary = new issue / money to issuer. Secondary = existing security traded / liquidity to investors.**

---

## 11. Equity vs Debt

| Equity | Debt |
|---|---|
| Investor becomes owner/shareholder | Investor becomes creditor/lender |
| Return may be dividend and capital gain | Return may be coupon/interest and capital gain |
| No guaranteed dividend | Contractual interest, subject to issuer ability and terms |
| Higher residual risk | Generally higher priority than equity in liquidation |
| No fixed maturity for ordinary equity | Debt normally has maturity (some instruments can be perpetual) |

**Exam trap:** Shareholder is an owner, bondholder is a creditor.

## 12. Bonds — Basic Terms

A **bond** is a debt instrument where an issuer borrows funds from investors and promises payments according to the terms.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Face value / Par value | Principal amount payable at maturity (unless terms differ) |
| Coupon | Interest rate applied to face value |
| Coupon payment | Cash interest paid at stated intervals |
| Maturity | Date principal is repaid |
| Issue price | Price when first sold |
| Market price | Price at which it trades now |
| Yield | Return measure based on price and cash flows |
| Premium | Market price above face value |
| Discount | Market price below face value |

### Example: coupon
Bond face value = ₹1,000  
Coupon rate = 7% per annum

Annual coupon = ₹1,000 × 7% = **₹70**.  
If coupons are paid half-yearly, each payment is typically ₹35 (subject to terms).

**Coupon rate is calculated on face value—not on the current market price.**

---

## 13. Bond price and yield relationship

This is a very important financial-awareness concept.

**Bond price and market yield generally move in opposite directions.**

- Market interest rates rise → existing fixed-coupon bond becomes less attractive → price falls → yield rises.
- Market interest rates fall → existing fixed-coupon bond becomes more attractive → price rises → yield falls.

### Example
Bond A pays fixed coupon ₹70/year on ₹1,000 face value (7% coupon).

If similar new bonds offer 8%, investors may not pay ₹1,000 for the old 7% bond. Its market price may fall below ₹1,000.

If new bonds offer only 6%, the old 7% bond becomes attractive and its market price may rise above ₹1,000.

**Mnemonic:** Rates ↑, bond prices ↓. Rates ↓, bond prices ↑.

### Why bond prices change
- RBI policy and market interest rates
- Inflation expectations
- Government borrowing/supply
- Demand and supply
- Credit rating / default risk
- Liquidity
- Maturity / duration
- Global market movements

---

## 14. Yield — important terms

### Coupon Yield
Annual coupon ÷ face value × 100.

Example: ₹70 coupon on ₹1,000 face value = **7%**.

### Current Yield
Annual coupon ÷ current market price × 100.

Example: Coupon ₹70, market price ₹950:

\[
\frac{70}{950}\times100\approx7.37\%
\]

### Yield to Maturity (YTM)
The estimated annualised return if the investor holds the bond to maturity and receives promised cash flows, considering:
- purchase price,
- coupon cash flows,
- maturity value,
- time remaining.

YTM is not simply coupon rate. It also depends on whether the bond was bought at a premium or discount and on the cash-flow timing/reinvestment assumptions.

### Yield curve
A graph showing yields of comparable debt securities across different maturities.

- **Normal/upward sloping:** longer maturities yield more than shorter maturities.
- **Flat:** yields across maturities are similar.
- **Inverted:** shorter maturities yield more than longer maturities.

The 10-year Government Security yield is commonly tracked as an important benchmark long-term interest rate in India.

## 15. Government Securities (G-Secs)

A **Government Security** is a tradable debt instrument issued by Central or State Government acknowledging its debt obligation.

### Central Government G-Secs
- T-Bills: short-term (less than one year).
- Dated securities / Government bonds: generally one year or more, carrying fixed or floating coupon according to terms.
- Central Government issues both T-Bills and dated G-Secs.

### State Development Loans (SDLs)
- Bonds/dated securities issued by State Governments.
- RBI conducts auctions on behalf of State Governments.
- Generally long-term debt.
- State Government borrowing instrument; not a Central Government T-Bill.

### Sovereign risk vs market risk
G-Secs in domestic currency are considered to carry negligible credit/default risk, but they are **not risk-free in every sense**:
- If market interest rates rise, their market price may fall.
- Selling before maturity may result in a capital loss.
- Inflation can reduce real purchasing power of fixed cash flows.

### G-Sec vs SDL
| G-Sec | SDL |
|---|---|
| Issued by Central Government | Issued by State Government |
| Central Government borrowing | State Government borrowing |
| RBI conducts issuance/auctions | RBI conducts auctions on behalf of states |
| Sovereign domestic debt | State-level government debt |

## 16. Types of Bonds

| Type | Feature |
|---|---|
| Fixed-rate bond | Coupon remains fixed |
| Floating-rate bond | Coupon changes based on reference rate/formula |
| Zero-coupon bond | No periodic coupon; generally issued at discount |
| Inflation-indexed bond | Cash flows/principal linked to inflation index as per terms |
| Green bond | Proceeds earmarked for eligible environmental projects |
| Callable bond | Issuer has right to redeem early under terms |
| Puttable bond | Investor has right to sell back early under terms |
| Perpetual bond | No conventional maturity date, subject to terms and risks |

**Trap:** Green bond is identified by use-of-proceeds/environmental purpose, not automatically by guaranteed low risk.

## 17. Auction of Government Securities

Government securities and T-Bills are issued through auctions conducted by RBI on behalf of the Government.

### Yield-based auction
- Generally used for a new security.
- Investors bid in yield terms.
- Bids are arranged from lower yield to higher yield.
- Cut-off yield is determined for notified amount.
- The cut-off yield generally becomes coupon rate for the new security under the auction structure.

### Price-based auction
- Often used for re-issuance of an existing security.
- Investors bid in price terms.
- Higher-price bids are generally preferred until notified amount is allotted.

### Competitive vs Non-Competitive Bidding

| Competitive | Non-Competitive |
|---|---|
| Investor quotes price/yield | Investor does not quote price/yield |
| Allotment based on auction bid/cut-off | Allotment at weighted-average price/yield from competitive auction, as applicable |
| Common for institutions/market participants | Helps eligible retail investors participate |

**Retail access:** RBI Retail Direct provides individual investors a route to participate in Government securities, subject to its terms and facilities.

---

## 18. Primary Dealers (PDs)

Primary Dealers are specialist intermediaries in the Government securities market.

Typical roles:
- Participate in primary auctions.
- Support Government borrowing.
- Provide liquidity and market-making in G-Secs.
- Maintain bid/offer quotes and participate in secondary-market activity, subject to regulatory obligations.

**Exam trap:** Primary Dealers are market intermediaries—not the issuer of Government securities.

## 20. Credit Rating & Spread

### Credit Rating
An opinion on the creditworthiness/default risk of an issuer or debt instrument from a rating agency.

Examples of rating agencies in India include CRISIL, ICRA, CARE Ratings and India Ratings.

- Higher rating generally indicates lower assessed credit risk.
- Lower rating generally means higher credit risk and may require higher yield.
- Rating is **not a guarantee** of repayment and can change.

### Credit Spread
Difference between yield on a corporate bond and comparable Government security yield.

Example:
- 5-year G-Sec yield = 6.5%
- 5-year corporate bond yield = 7.4%
- Spread = **0.9 percentage point = 90 basis points (bps)**

1 basis point (bp) = 0.01 percentage point.  
100 bps = 1 percentage point.

## 21. Forex & Derivatives — quick overview

### Forex Market
Market for exchange of currencies. Exchange rates are influenced by demand/supply, interest rates, inflation, trade flows, capital flows and policy.

Example: USD/INR tells how many Indian rupees are required for one US dollar.

### Derivatives
Contracts whose value depends on an underlying asset/rate/index.

- **Forward:** customised OTC contract to transact in future at agreed terms.
- **Futures:** standardised exchange-traded contract.
- **Option:** buyer gets a right, not obligation, to buy/sell according to terms; option writer has obligation if exercised.
- **Swap:** exchange of cash-flow streams according to contract (e.g., interest-rate swap).

Uses: hedging, price discovery, risk transfer; derivatives can also be used for speculation and carry significant risk.

## 22. Why financial markets matter to banks (IT Officer lens)

Banks are not only deposit-taking and lending institutions. Their treasury and financial-market operations also:
- Manage liquidity.
- Invest in G-Secs/approved instruments.
- Maintain regulatory requirements such as SLR as applicable.
- Manage interest-rate risk and Asset-Liability Management (ALM).
- Participate in money markets for short-term funding.
- Monitor market risk, valuation and settlement.

### IT systems and controls
- Market feeds and benchmark-rate integration.
- Accurate bond pricing and valuation.
- Trade capture and maker-checker approval.
- Position, limit and risk monitoring.
- Settlement instructions and reconciliation.
- Audit logs and access controls.
- Business continuity and disaster recovery.
- Prevent duplicate trades/settlement instructions.

Try these before looking at the key.

A. Quotes exact yield  B. Does not quote yield/price and receives allotment based on auction's weighted average  C. Cannot participate  D. Buys only equity

### Answer key with explanations
1. **B** — Money market is short-term, generally up to one year.  
2. **B** — Call money is overnight unsecured liquidity.  
3. **A** — CP is an unsecured negotiable promissory note.  
4. **B** — Eligible banks and specified FIs issue CDs.  
5. **B** — T-Bills pay no coupon; discount creates return.  
6. **B** — 91, 182 and 364 days.  
7. **B** — CMBs address temporary cash mismatches and mature under 91 days.  
8. **B** — Repo involves sale and agreed repurchase; economically secured borrowing.  
9. **B** — Primary issue delivers new funds to issuer.  
10. **B** — Bond prices and yields move inversely.  
11. **B** — Coupon is based on face value.  
12. **B** — SDLs are State Government securities.  
13. **C** — A 91-day T-Bill is money-market instrument.  
14. **B** — 7.4 − 6.5 = 0.9 percentage point = 90 bps.  
15. **B** — Non-competitive bidders don't quote yield/price; allotment uses auction outcome under the scheme.
